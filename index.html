Bayesian calibration across thermal, viscoelastic and hysteretic models

PhD, Chapters 2 and 3. Chapter 3 is published in the International Journal for Uncertainty Quantification (2026). Chapter 2 is part of the DAMOCLES project at EAISI, TU/e.

Key result. Calibration worked for the thermal, viscoelastic and Jiles–Atherton models, and exposed the Bouc–Wen model as unable to describe the material.

  • Sampler comparison, Int. J. Uncertainty Quantification (2026)
  • Related conference paper on the squeeze flow problem, Physical Sciences Forum (2025)

Problem

Engineering simulations combine conservation laws, which are reliable, with constitutive equations, which describe how a material responds and are much less certain. Their parameters come from laboratory measurements that are noisy and limited. The question here: how well does Bayesian calibration work across very different kinds of material behavior, and what limits it?

Approach

I applied one Bayesian pipeline to three experiments: heat conduction in a paraffin wax column, large amplitude oscillatory shear of a shampoo in a rheometer (a nonlinear viscoelastic fluid, Giesekus model), and magnetic hysteresis measured with an Epstein frame (Jiles–Atherton and Bouc–Wen models). Priors come from the literature and preliminary estimates, the likelihood from the measurement error, and the posterior is explored with adaptive Metropolis–Hastings MCMC. Each calibrated model is then tested on independent data: a different source temperature, excitation frequency or field amplitude. I ran all the Bayesian analyses, and the heat conduction rig was my own design.

p(θ | d) ∝ p(d | θ) p(θ),   with   d = f(θ) + ε Measurements d update the parameters θ of the physics model f, given measurement error ε. The answer is a distribution, not a single value.

Results

  • Heat conduction. Predictions stayed within about 0.23 °C RMSD of the calibration data, with good agreement on the independent run.
  • Viscoelastic shampoo. The stress response was reproduced closely on the calibration data (0.5 Hz). On the validation data (1 Hz) the error grew to a few Pa, but the main features of the response were still reproduced.
  • Magnetic hysteresis. The Jiles–Atherton model reproduced the loops on calibration and validation data. The Bouc–Wen model did not, even after calibration.

The last result is the most useful one. When the model cannot describe the physics, calibration cannot rescue it, and the uncertainty bounds only describe a model that is wrong. Calibration makes this visible instead of hiding it.

Choosing the sampler

A related question is which MCMC algorithm to use. With colleagues (Rinkens, Silva et al.), I compared Metropolis–Hastings, the Affine Invariant Stretch Move and the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) on two real experiments: the thermal conduction problem with a finite element model, and a Newtonian squeeze flow with a semi-analytical model. This comparison did not include the viscoelastic or hysteresis problems. Convergence was measured as the KL divergence to a reference posterior, next to the usual Gelman–Rubin and effective sample size diagnostics. My part was the heat conduction setting and the analysis of the Metropolis–Hastings and NUTS samplers. NUTS and the Stretch Move sampler used existing Julia packages.

  • At a fixed number of samples, NUTS converged best.
  • Per model evaluation, NUTS lost to the other two, because each step needs many evaluations including gradients. For the finite element thermal model it gave no advantage per independent sample.
  • The default Stretch Move setup suits a broad range of problems, while Metropolis–Hastings needs proposal tuning that can cost as much as the sampling itself.
  • The standard diagnostics tracked the KL divergence qualitatively, but not in a clear quantitative way.

The right sampler depends on how expensive the model is and whether gradients are available.

Limits

The thermal case is the easy one: a linear model with informative transient data, so its good results should not be read as typical. The conclusions are drawn from these specific systems, not from a general proof.

Discretization-optimized calibration

PhD, Chapter 4. First author. Preprint on arXiv, under review.

Key result. About 42 times less computation than a fixed fine reference on the full real dataset, and about 30,500 times less on steady-state data.

  • arXiv:2604.01101

Problem

The thermal conductivity of paraffin wax depends on temperature, so the unknown is a curve k(T), not a single number. Estimating it from noisy sensor data needs a numerical model, and that model comes with choices: how fine the mesh and time steps are, and how many segments describe the curve. Too coarse and the estimate is biased. Too fine and the calibration takes needlessly long.

Approach

I developed an algorithm that makes these choices from the data itself. In an inner loop it refines the mesh and time step, guided by gradient-based optimization, and stops when the numerical error is no longer larger than the measurement noise (Morozov's discrepancy principle). In an outer loop it adds segments to the conductivity curve only while the information criteria (BIC and DIC) say the extra complexity is justified. MCMC then gives the uncertainty of the final curve, reported as 99% credible intervals.

ρcp ∂T/∂t = ∂/∂x ( k(T) ∂T/∂x ) + (2hside / R) (T∞ − T)
d = f + εpred + εmeas The unknown is the curve k(T). The numerical error εpred is kept small relative to the measurement noise εmeas, and no smaller than that requires.

Results

  • Synthetic data. Checked against a very fine reference solution (128 elements, 4096 time steps): the relative difference in predicted temperatures was about 0.01%, and the largest deviation was 0.19 °C.
  • Real rig data, full dataset. A 14 hour experiment with four sensors. With transient and steady-state data the algorithm chose 24 elements, 512 time steps and 8 curve segments, using about 42 times less computation than the fixed fine reference.
  • Real rig data, steady state only. It chose 8 elements, 2 time steps and 2 segments, about 30,500 times less computation, and the predictions still agreed with the measurements.
  • Computation is counted in solver work units, which tracked CPU time.

The algorithm adapts to how informative the data are, instead of applying one fixed resolution to every dataset.

Limits

The real-data run illustrates the algorithm: the other physical properties were calibrated beforehand and held fixed. The method is tied to quantities that relate closely to the likelihood, here the sensor temperatures, and it was shown on a low-dimensional one-dimensional problem. Larger problems would raise the cost, which is where surrogate models come in.

Goal-oriented adaptivity with surrogates

PhD, Chapter 5. Not yet published.

Key result. About 150 times faster on a problem with 2 unknowns, and about 8 times faster on a harder problem with 4 unknowns, both including the cost of building the surrogate.

Problem

MCMC needs the forward model thousands of times, and each finite element solve is expensive. But only the sensor readings enter the likelihood. If those readings are accurate, the rest of the solution does not need to be.

Approach

I combined two ideas. Offline, goal-oriented adaptive finite elements estimate the error in the sensor readings and refine the mesh only where it matters: near steep gradients and near the sensors. This produces training data cheaply. Online, Gaussian process surrogates of the sensor readings replace the finite element solves inside MCMC. The uncertainty from the discretization and from the surrogate is added to the likelihood, so a less trustworthy surrogate widens the posterior instead of silently biasing it. The test problem is a block of material with a hidden inclusion, whose position, size and orientation are inferred from four temperature sensors.

Σ = Σnoise + Σsurrogate In the likelihood, the surrogate's own uncertainty adds to the measurement noise.

Results

  • For the same number of unknowns, adaptive refinement reached smaller errors than uniform refinement, at the optimal convergence rate.
  • Circular inclusion (2 unknowns). About 150 times faster than finite element MCMC: about 20 s against about 3,100 s for 10,000 samples, including the cost of generating the training data.
  • Elliptical inclusion (4 unknowns). About 8 times faster, including the full offline cost. This is a conservative estimate because the finite element model here is cheap. Larger gains are expected for more expensive models.
  • Position and size were recovered, with the true values inside the 95% credible intervals. The orientation is hard to determine from four sensors, and the posterior said so with a wide interval.
  • More training data and finer meshes improved the surrogate, with diminishing returns. Multi-output Gaussian processes gave only modest gains over independent ones.

There are two speedups here, not one: 150 times when the problem is small, 8 times when it is harder.

Limits

The benchmarks are two dimensional. The link between finite element error estimates and surrogate uncertainty is simplified. Gaussian process training sets grow quickly with the number of parameters, so larger problems would need sparse methods.

Bayesian design of tumor ablation with focused ultrasound

MSc research. First author. Published in Numerical Heat Transfer, Part B: Fundamentals (2024), with M. Alaeian and H. Orlande.

Key result. For a 3×5 mm tumor, two heating sessions instead of one raised the damage probability above 94% across the whole tumor and reduced damage to healthy tissue.

  • Numerical Heat Transfer, Part B (2024)

Problem

High intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) can destroy a tumor without surgery by heating a small focal region. Treatment planning relies on simulation, but tissue properties are known only roughly. Which transducer position and heating times ablate the tumor reliably while sparing healthy tissue?

Approach

I chained three simulations: nonlinear ultrasound propagation (the k-Wave toolbox in MATLAB), heat conduction driven by the absorbed ultrasound, and Arrhenius thermal damage, which gives the probability that cells at each location are destroyed. The design problem is then posed as Bayesian inference. The transducer position and the heating time of each session are the unknowns, tissue properties carry Gaussian uncertainty, and the likelihood encodes the desired outcome: a damage probability near 100% in the tumor, an open margin around it, and low damage elsewhere (beta distributions). A two-block Metropolis–Hastings sampler optimizes the design variables while the tissue properties only contribute uncertainty.

Ω = ∫ A exp( −Ea / RT(t′) ) dt′,   Y = 1 − e−Ω Y is the probability that the cells at a location are thermally damaged. It is computed from the temperature history T.

Results

  • 1×5 mm tumor, one session. Heating of about 15 s with the transducer centered. The damage probability was above 94% in the tumor and below 52% in the healthy tissue region. The peak temperature was about 62 °C, which avoids boiling.
  • 3×5 mm tumor. One session left parts of the tumor under-treated while damaging healthy tissue above it. Two sessions raised the probability above 94% in the whole tumor and reduced the collateral damage.
  • 5×1 mm tumor. Four sessions (12 design variables), with the focus moving across the tumor. The tumor probability was above about 90%. Healthy tissue along the beam path still reached about 80% in places, so the shape and position of a tumor limit what a fixed transducer can do.
  • The design variables were estimated with small uncertainty (about 0.1 mm and 0.1 s in the single-session case). Uncertainty in the pressure, heat source, temperature and damage fields came as a by-product.

Limits

The tumors are idealized two dimensional shapes, not patient data. One single-session design took about 90 hours on a workstation, so computational cost is the main obstacle to practical use.
